# Tally: report

Job family: **finance & accounting**. Data: 12 real LinkedIn postings (Kaggle *arshkon*) and 295 real resumes (Kaggle
*snehaanbhawal*). Golden set: 180 labelled (job, resume) pairs, with **3 dev jobs (45 pairs)** used for every tuning
choice and **9 test jobs (135 pairs)** used only for reporting. Every number below comes from `results/*.json` and
is regenerated by `make reproduce`.

> **Label status.** The 180 relevance/decision labels are **human**: written blind from the text packets, without
> system output (`golden/labels.csv`). The 30 explanation ratings the judge is compared with are **human** too
> (`golden/explanation_ratings.csv`). Claude Sonnet drafts of both are kept as second raters (§3, §7). Still
> AI-drafted: the JD/extraction gold (§6). The headline numbers use Claude Sonnet as the pipeline's LLM; §11
> re-runs everything on two Gemini models, including the production setting, `gemini-3.5-flash-lite`.

## 1. Problem framing

A recruiter with 15–300 applicants wants two things from screening: a **short list they can trust**, and the
guarantee that **nobody good is quietly dropped**. So Tally only ever *promotes* (Shortlist) or *routes to a human*
(Needs review / Low priority), and every line of its reasoning is a checklist item with a quote. Missing a good
candidate is costed at 5× shortlisting a weak one.

**Not built**: auto-reject; an LLM that outputs a score; OCR/PDF parsing (the data is text); multi-family support;
learning-to-rank (180 labels is not enough to train on and still evaluate honestly).

## 2. System

Posting → LLM checklist (4–10 requirements: must/nice, category, years/degree, source quote). Resume → clean (NFKC,
invisible characters, PII mask, injection regex) → LLM profile (roles with dates, degrees, certifications).
For each requirement: years and degree are compared **in code**; everything else is graded by the LLM **from the 5
resume passages BM25 retrieves for it**, with a verbatim quote that code verifies (no match → zero credit + review).
Score = Σ weight·credit / Σ weight (must ×2, nice ×1; met 1, partial ½, not met 0).

**Lanes.** *Shortlist*: score ≥ 0.525, at most one must-have not fully met, no blocking flag. *Low priority*: score
< 0.35 with a must-have missing and no blocking flag. *Needs review*: everything else, with reason codes
(`MUST_NOT_MET`, `MUST_PARTIAL`, `BORDERLINE`, `INJECTION`, `KEYWORD_STUFFING`, `UNVERIFIED_QUOTE`, `DATES_UNCLEAR`,
`THIN_RESUME`; `LOCATION` is information only). The one allowed gap is always named in the explanation as
"Ask about: …".

## 3. Golden set

* **Sampling** (`tally/golden.py`, independent of the system): per job, 5 BM25-top and 5 random from the
  ACCOUNTANT/FINANCE pool, 3 from near categories (banking, consulting, sales, business development), and 2 hard cases
  (the most BM25-similar non-finance resume, and a finance resume whose headline matches the title but ranks outside
  BM25's top 30). Each resume is used for at most 2 jobs: 129 distinct resumes.
* **Labels** (`golden/rubric.md`): relevance 0–3 and a decision (advance → shortlist, escalate → review), with a
  note per pair. They were written by a person from text-only packets, with no access to scores. Distribution:
  0: 60, 1: 48, 2: 42, 3: 30, with **63 ideal shortlists** (all 30 relevance-3 pairs and 33 of the relevance-2 pairs).
* **Second rater.** A Claude Sonnet session labelled the same 180 pairs under the same rubric, also blind
  (`results/label_agreement.json`). Relevance weighted κ **0.90** (77% exact, 100% within ±1). Decision κ **0.90**
  (95.6% agreement). The disagreements are systematic, not random. The human scored relevance one point lower on 39
  pairs (mean 1.23 vs 1.43), yet shortlisted 8 relevance-2 candidates the model sent to review. Every model shortlist
  is also a human shortlist. So the human is stricter about fit but more willing to let a phone call settle one gap.
* **Hard cases** include overqualified directors for associate roles, CPA *candidates* for CPA-required roles,
  foreign credentials (Philippine CPA, ACCA), finance titles over non-finance duties, and career changers.

## 4. Results against baselines (test: 9 jobs, 135 pairs)

| system | NDCG@5 [95% CI] | P@5 | Spearman | shortlisted | shortlist precision | recall | sent to a human | cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tally** | **0.80** [0.70, 0.89] | **0.64** | **0.69** | 37 | **0.59** | 0.55 | 73% | 105 |
| TF-IDF cosine | 0.76 [0.67, 0.84] | 0.60 | 0.61 | 82 | 0.39 | 0.80 | 39% | 90 |
| keyword overlap | 0.70 [0.59, 0.80] | 0.62 | 0.54 | 103 | 0.36 | 0.93 | 24% | **81** |

CIs bootstrap whole jobs. Baseline thresholds were tuned on dev with the same cost rule.

* **Ranking is clearly better.** The checklist separates "is an accountant" from "has what this posting asks for".
  Lexical baselines can't: every accounting resume shares the vocabulary.
* **Decisions lose on the cost metric** (105 vs 90 and 81). There are 40 good test candidates; Tally shortlists 37
  people and gets 22 right (precision 0.59, recall 0.55). At 5:1 cost, every missed good candidate outweighs five
  wrong shortlists. So the baselines win by shortlisting 61–76% of all applicants, which a recruiter would not call a
  shortlist. Tally is the most precise list of the three, but it misses too many good people, and the cost rule I
  chose up front says that matters more. That is the main open problem (§8, §10).
* **No good candidate is buried.** None of the 40 went to Low priority; the 18 that were missed are all in Needs
  review, with reason codes.
* **Per job**: NDCG@5 is ≥ 0.85 on 5 of 9 jobs (J12 accounting manager 1.00, J03 payroll/AP 0.92), but J07 FP&A
  manager scores 0.47 (§8.1) and J10 payroll 0.65.

## 5. Decision policy and how it was chosen

The first policy shortlisted only when **every** must-have was fully met. Its test result was precision 0.70,
recall 0.21, 93% sent to review and cost 133, measured on the AI-drafted labels available at the time. On the human
labels it would be 9/10 correct, recall 0.23 and cost 156. That is safe but useless, and worse than both baselines.
Recall was the problem: a single *partial* must-have (a "CPA candidate", a tool listed but not used) blocked the
shortlist, while the rubric lets a recruiter shortlist someone whose one gap "a phone call settles".

I then compared gate variants **on the dev jobs only**, with the threshold re-tuned on dev for each. The choice
was made on the draft labels. The table below re-runs it on the human labels (`results/thresholds.json`), and the
ranking of the options is the same:

| gate (dev, 45 pairs, human labels) | threshold | cost | precision | recall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| all must-haves met (v1) | 0.725 | 90 | 1.00 | 0.22 |
| **≤ 1 must-have not fully met (chosen)** | 0.525 | 35 | 1.00 | 0.70 |
| ≤ 2 not fully met | 0.40 | 13 | 0.88 | 0.91 |
| score only | 0.45 | 15 | 0.81 | 0.91 |

I chose the one-gap rule over the cheaper options because a shortlist missing two must-haves is not one a recruiter
can trust, and it matches the rubric. The human labels make the trade-off sharper: on dev, two gaps would cost a third
as much. I did **not** switch after seeing the human test results, because that would be tuning on test. Whether the
recruiter wants "precise" or "complete" is a product decision (§10). **Disclosure:** the v1 test numbers had been
seen before the policy change, so the test figures are not a fully clean held-out result. v1 and the draft-label
results are archived in `results/v1_all_musts_gate/` and `results/archive_sonnet_draft_labels/`.

## 7. Explanations and the LLM judge

Explanations are assembled by code from the graded checklist ("Shows 3 of 4 must-haves… Evidence: … Gaps: … Ask
about: …"), so they can't invent anything the grader didn't say. **All 30 sampled explanations' quotes are verified
verbatim against the resume** (0 of 180 graded pairs had an unverifiable quote).

The **judge** (`tally/prompts.py: judge`) reads the full resume, while the screener only saw retrieved passages, so it
can catch evidence the screener missed. Agreement with the **human reviewer** on 30 test explanations, stratified by
lane (`results/judge.json`):

| dimension | human mean | judge mean | weighted κ | within ±1 | Spearman |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| grounded | 3.07 | 3.13 | **0.52** | 90% | 0.47 |
| gaps correct | 3.73 | 3.37 | 0.14 | 80% | 0.09 |
| actionable | 3.57 | 3.07 | 0.29 | 97% | 0.37 |

* **The judge is usable for groundedness only.** It matches the human's mean and ranks explanations moderately well
  (κ 0.52). On gap correctness and actionability it is close to chance, and it is harsher than the human on both.
* **An AI reference would have flattered it.** Against the earlier Sonnet-written reference ratings, the same judge
  scored κ 0.62 / 0.48 / 0.38. The human and that Sonnet reference agree only at κ 0.21 / 0.19 / 0.01. Same-family
  agreement is not human agreement.
* **What the human caught.** Explanations are quote-safe, but the *claims* are not always right. The commonest
  theme (17 of 30 comments touch on it) is how years are used: general finance years presented as role-specific
  tenure ("4+ years of audit"). That is
  failure mode 1 seen from the reader's side. Two code defects also surfaced. (a) The degree check compares level
  but not field, so a civil-engineering master's "met" both "Bachelor's in accounting" and "Master of Tax"
  (J05/C28973180, grounded 1). (b) An explanation reported "9 years in finance roles" while the same card flagged
  the role dates as unusable (J07/C14181049). Both are listed for the next iteration (§10). They were not fixed after
  the test evaluation.

## 8. Failure analysis (test set, real examples)

1. **"Years in a similar role" counted as years in any finance role.** The profile marks each role only as finance /
   not finance, so years requirements are judged too generously. Example: J07 FP&A manager (asks 7 years including
   leadership). Four candidates the labeller rated 0–1 were shortlisted, among them accountants, a business
   consultant and a purchasing manager, all on general finance years (e.g. C13130984, an accounting clerk who became
   an accountant, scored 0.86). That drags J07 to NDCG@5 0.47. *Hypothesis*: extract
   per-role function tags (FP&A, payroll, audit, tax…) and match years to the requirement's function.
2. **The one allowed gap can be the core of the job.** J10 payroll specialist shortlisted two CPAs with no payroll
   experience (C21794875 rated 1, C16426777 rated 0): the only unmet must-have was "payroll laws", but the rest of the checklist
   (degree, Excel, years) carried the score. *Hypothesis*: the title skill (payroll, tax, audit) should be a hard
   requirement that the one-gap rule can never waive.
3. **Retrieval misses evidence phrased differently.** J04 (AR accountant, state agency) has the most misses: 6 of
   its good candidates went to review. C24703009 (relevance 3: Alaska statewide accounting system, multiple funding
   sources) got "GAAP not shown" and "MS Office not shown" because no passage contained those words, and was sent to
   review at 0.45. 18 good test candidates were not shortlisted, and none went to Low priority. *Hypothesis*: always
   include the skills section as a passage, and accept implied evidence for generic tools.
4. **Checklist extraction promotes niche or trivial items to must-haves.** J02's "Timberline (Sage 300)" was graded
   as a strict must-have. It is a gap for all four J02 accountants the labeller would advance but Tally sent to review
   (C27637576, C49204385, C53640713, C78257294, all relevance 2). For C78257294 it is the only gap, and it pulls the
   score to 0.50, just under the line. The other three also have a second gap (no bachelor's, or "5+ years in a similar
   role"). In their notes the labeller accepted QuickBooks, Dynamics or PeopleSoft as "close enough".
   An Office/Excel must-have appears in 6 of the 12 checklists and fails on resumes that never name the tool. Must-have
   recall against the gold is 0.63. *Hypothesis*: give the checklist step an "equivalent accepted" field and drop
   tool requirements every applicant meets.
5. **Credentials in progress.** "CPA candidate" and "CPA-eligible" grade as *partial*. Under the one-gap rule, J12
   shortlisted four CPA candidates (C28969385, C23416654, C15289348, C29821051) for a role requiring a professional
   accreditation; the labeller rated all four 2 but escalated them ("the CPA is not yet held, which the JD requires").
   *Hypothesis*: treat credentials as binary (held / not held),
   with "in progress" shown as a note.

**Manipulation** (synthetic edits of 4 low-fit real resumes, `results/adversarial.json`). A visible injection and a
zero-width-split injection were flagged 8/8. A paraphrased claim ("the committee already verified this applicant") was
**not** flagged (0/4) but also moved no scores. Pasting the job's requirements as a skills line raised scores by
+0.31 and shortlisted 2 of 4 until a stuffing check was added: it flags any single line that contains ≥ 50% of the
requirements. That check now flags 4/4 with 0 shortlisted. On the real golden resumes the highest such ratio is
0.20, so it changes no golden decision. A paraphrased stuffing attack would get past it.

**Fairness** (`results/fairness.json`, 6 plausible applicants on 6 test jobs, chosen by BM25 not by score). The swaps
were a name line (6 names across gender and ethnicity), a gender cue (Girl Scouts vs Boy Scouts treasurer) and the
college on an added degree line (Harvard / Arizona State / Howard / Grand Canyon).

* **Gender cue and college: score spread 0.000 and 0 lane flips on both models.**
* **Names: a real leak, found by the Gemini run and now fixed.** A name on the first line merged into the first
  retrieved passage ("Emily Walsh ACCOUNTANT Professional Summary…"), so it *did* reach the grader. Under Sonnet the
  answers happened not to change. Under Gemini, one applicant (J04/C23734441) scored 0.94 and was shortlisted as
  Greg Baker, Lakisha Washington, Jamal Robinson or Priya Patel, but 0.72 and sent to review as Emily Walsh or Wei Zhang
  (max spread 0.22, 1/6 bases flipped). The mechanism was prompt sensitivity, not a judgement about names: with the
  name in the prompt, Gemini stitched two resume fragments into one "quote", verification rejected it, and the
  candidate went to review. The outcome still depended on the name, though, so it counts as a fairness failure.
* **Fix:** a first line that looks like a person's name is masked to `[NAME]` before any LLM call (Title Case job
  titles are kept; no real resume in the sample is affected, so no headline number moved). **After the fix: spread
  0.000 and 0 flips on both models** (pre-fix results in `results/pre_name_mask/`).
* **Colleges, on the production model:** after the name fix, Gemini 3.5-flash-lite still moved one applicant
  from review to shortlist when the degree line said Harvard instead of Arizona State, Howard or Grand Canyon (§11).
  Institution masking is the open fix.
* **Limits:** names elsewhere in a resume (a signature, a reference) are not masked; there are only 6 bases; and the
  test only proves the pipeline is blind to these edits, not that the labels or the postings are unbiased.

## 9. What is misleading about the headline number?

* **NDCG@5 0.80 is mostly "finance beats non-finance".** Every job's pool contains 3–5 resumes from other fields
  (near categories, hard look-alikes), so any decent system puts them last. The harder question, which accountant
  fits *this* accounting job, is where J07 (0.47) and J10 (0.65) show the real weakness.
* **The decision policy was revised after seeing test results** (§5). The rule was chosen on dev, but I only looked
  for a new rule because v1 looked bad on test.
* **Dev is tiny.** 45 pairs and 23 good candidates set the threshold. Dev precision is 1.00, test precision is 0.59,
  and the bootstrap CI on NDCG@5 spans 0.70–0.89.
* **The labels changed the story, so the label source matters.** On the AI-drafted labels Tally narrowly beat both
  baselines on cost (82 vs 85/88). On the human labels it loses (105 vs 90/81), even though the two label sets agree
  at κ 0.90. A few systematic differences (the human advancing "one gap a phone call settles" more often) were enough
  to flip the decision result. Any small golden set is that sensitive.
* **The extraction gold is still AI-made**, so §6 measures agreement with Sonnet, not with a person. §7 shows how much
  that can matter: the judge's κ against Sonnet ratings was roughly double its κ against the human.
* **The numbers are for one model.** Swapping to Gemini changes the lane of 39% of pairs. 3.1-flash-lite keeps the
  ranking (NDCG@5 0.80, cost 110); 3.5-flash-lite, the production setting, drops to 0.75 (cost 116) and showed a
  college-prestige flip (§11). A "the agent is good enough" claim is a claim about a model + prompt
  pair, not about the design.
* **Cost differences are coarse.** One more or one fewer missed good candidate moves the cost by 5, and the
  15-point gap to TF-IDF is three candidates.
* **The labels reward the checklist's view of the world.** The rubric asks "does the resume show each must-have",
  which is the same question the system answers. A recruiter who weighs trajectory or company quality would label
  differently.

## 10. With one more week

1. **Recover recall without giving up precision.** The 18 missed good candidates all sit in Needs review with
   reason codes. Fixes 3 and 4 below target most of them, and the recruiter could choose the gate (one gap vs two) per
   job: "precise list" or "complete list". Fix the two explanation defects the human found (degree field ignored;
   years stated when dates are unusable), and get a human-checked JD/extraction gold so §6 stops being measured
   against a model.
2. **Make the checklist model-stable.** Most cross-model disagreement starts at checklist extraction (Gemini marks
   7 of J02's 8 requirements as must-haves, Sonnet 3 of 5). A recruiter-confirmed checklist (one click per posting)
   would remove the biggest source of variance and of error at once.
3. Fix failure modes 1, 2 and 5 (function-tagged years, a non-waivable title skill, binary credentials), then evaluate
   on 4 *new* labelled jobs, because the current test set is no longer clean.
4. Add the skills section to every retrieval and measure the recall change against judge-flagged "missed evidence".
5. A larger fairness suite: 30 bases, swaps inside the education field and the profile itself, and a check that
   extracted degree/years don't change when the name does.

## 11. Cross-model check: Claude Sonnet vs two Gemini models

The same code, prompts, golden set (human labels) and policy, with every LLM step answered by each model in turn.
The threshold was re-tuned on the dev jobs per model and landed on 0.525 each time. The production setting is now
`gemini-3.5-flash-lite`, chosen because `3.1-flash-lite` was too often overloaded (503) to rely on. Sources:
`results/gemini/` (3.5-flash-lite), `results/gemini_3.1_flash_lite/`, `results/model_comparison.json`.

| test (9 jobs, 135 pairs) | Sonnet | Gemini 3.1-flash-lite | Gemini 3.5-flash-lite |
|---|---|---|---|
| NDCG@5 [95% CI] | **0.80** [0.70, 0.89] | **0.80** [0.73, 0.87] | 0.75 [0.63, 0.84] |
| P@5 / Spearman | **0.64** / 0.69 | 0.62 / **0.71** | 0.58 / 0.62 |
| shortlisted / correct (of 40 good) | 37 / 22 | 42 / 22 | 54 / 23 |
| shortlist precision / recall | **0.59** / 0.55 | 0.52 / 0.55 | 0.43 / **0.57** |
| sent to a human | 73% | 69% | 60% |
| cost (5 × missed + 1 × wrong) | **105** | 110 | 116 |
| dev cost (what was tuned) | 35 | 27 | 26 |

| extraction vs gold | Sonnet | 3.1-flash-lite | 3.5-flash-lite |
|---|---|---|---|
| JD must-haves F1 | 0.72 | 0.71 | 0.72 |
| certifications F1 | 0.86 | 0.86 | **0.93** |
| highest education level | **95%** | 81% | 90% |
| finance-relevant years MAE (within 1 y) | **1.55 y (76%)** | 3.14 y (52%) | 2.56 y (57%) |

**Agreement with Sonnet (all 180 pairs).** 3.5-flash-lite: score Spearman 0.84, same lane 61%, 48 shortlist flips.
3.1-flash-lite: 0.84, 61%, 45 flips. Both Gemini models mark more requirements as must-haves on some postings (J07
6 vs 2; J02 5–7 vs 3). Dev cost improves with each Gemini model, but test cost gets worse: the threshold tuned on 3 dev
jobs does not carry over, and 3.5-flash-lite shortlists 54 people to find 23 good ones.

**Fairness on 3.5-flash-lite: one prestige flip.** On J09 (senior internal auditor), base C26231609 scored 0.80 and was
**shortlisted with "Harvard University"** on its degree line, but 0.70 and sent to review with Arizona State, Howard
or Grand Canyon (college max spread 0.10, 1/6 bases flipped). The degree line was retrieved as evidence for "knowledge
of accounting principles, COSO, SOX, PCAOB". With the *same* supporting quote, the model graded it "met" next to
Harvard and "partial" next to the others. Name and gender-cue swaps showed no movement. Sonnet and 3.1-flash-lite
showed no movement on any swap. **Mitigation (not yet applied):** mask institution names before grading, the way
names are masked (§8). That changes the grading prompts, so it means re-running all three models. It is the first item
before 3.5-flash-lite goes to production.

**Judge vs the human reviewer** (weighted κ grounded / gaps / actionable): Sonnet 0.52 / 0.14 / 0.29;
3.1-flash-lite 0.31 / 0.11 / 0.22; 3.5-flash-lite −0.01 / −0.09 / 0.09. 3.5-flash-lite is also extremely lenient
(means 4.2–4.7 vs the human's 3.1–3.7). As prompted, only the Sonnet judge is usable, and only for groundedness.

**Manipulation** holds on all three models: injections 8/8 flagged, stuffing 4/4 flagged, 0 shortlisted.

**Takeaways.** (1) The checklist design beats the lexical baselines on ranking with Sonnet and 3.1-flash-lite, but
3.5-flash-lite (0.75) is within the TF-IDF CI. (2) No model beats the baselines on decision cost under the human
labels. (3) Fairness depends on the model, not only on the design: the same pipeline was blind to college names under
two models and not under the third. Every model change needs the fairness suite re-run, which this one did.
